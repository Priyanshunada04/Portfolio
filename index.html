01Concept projectFintechConversational AI

Flow: banking that disappears

AI money tools for Gen Z that show up inside iMessage, Instagram and Uber at the moment someone is about to spend, instead of asking them to open a separate banking app.

My role
End to end: research, platform architecture, interaction design
Deliverables
21-screen clickable prototype across all three products, architecture docs, React case-study site
Surfaces
iMessage, lock screen, widgets
Status
Concept, not built for production

The problem

Friends already sort out shared costs in group chats. Then they leave the chat to pay in one app, check their balance in another, and save toward trips in a spreadsheet.

Banking apps show up after the money is spent. The decision itself happens somewhere else, and every switch is a chance to drop the task, forget who owes what, or overspend without noticing.

My approach

Instead of one more app, I designed three small tools that each appear at a specific moment: right after a shared bill, right before a purchase, and while a group plans a trip.

They sit on one shared layer, so each can pass the user to the next one at the right time. Every tool shows its suggestion, offers a choice, and gets out of the way.

Three products, one shared layer

  • Reactive · iMessage Flow Split Turns "dinner was $94.50, split 3 ways" into a payment request without leaving the chat.
  • Proactive · lock screen Flow Safe Forecasts what's safe to spend over the next 7, 14 and 30 days and nudges before a purchase.
  • Social · group chat Flow Together Group savings pots with scheduled contributions, milestones and opt-in accountability.
Shared layerProfilesBank and card APIsPredictionNotificationsSecurity

Handoffs between products

  1. Safe→SplitSafe warns that dinner will put you over budget. You go anyway, so Split offers: "Split this $85 to stay on track?"
  2. Together→SplitThe trip pot hits $2,000 but the booking is $2,340. Split offers to divide the $340 gap four ways.
  3. Split→SafeYou and Sarah split costs eight times a month, so Flow suggests a shared expense pot instead.

Inside each product

Designing for "no"

Most payment flows are drawn for the happy path. I spent as much time on what happens when a friend doesn't pay.

If someone hasn't opened the request, Flow doesn't nag them on its own. It asks the organizer whether to send a gentle nudge, because that's a social call, not a system one.

If someone declines, the screen says plainly that no money moved and offers three ways out: cover their share, nudge again or cancel. Nothing is left half finished.

A heads-up that shows its math

Safe forecasts what's safe to spend from recurring bills, income timing and a savings goal, and speaks up only at a decision point, where the person already is: the lock screen, an iMessage thread or a widget.

Every number explains itself. "Why $27 today" walks through the math, says how sure Flow is about each part, and lets people fix a wrong bill or paycheck date on the spot.

Every nudge can be waved off, tuned or paused. When Flow learns from skipped nudges, it says what it changed and offers an undo.

Saving as a group, without calling anyone out

Flow notices a group goal in the chat and offers a pot. Friends choose to join, then contribute on a schedule or with round-ups.

Celebrate in public, nudge in private. The group sees progress and milestones. Each person's amount stays private unless they share it, and "you're $40 behind" goes only to the person it's about.

Money moves only when the group agrees. Spending the pot needs 3 of 4 approvals, anyone can cancel, and requests expire in 48 hours.

Key decisions

  1. Build into the chat, not a new app.The conversation is where the decision happens. The cost: Flow is limited by what each platform lets a third party show, so every card had to work in a small space.
  2. Explain the number, then let people fix it.A forecast people can't question is one they stop trusting. Safe shows its math and confidence, and corrections update it right away.
  3. Celebrate progress, don't shame.Together marks milestones and keeps each person's contribution opt-in, instead of calling out whoever is behind.
  4. Show money status in words, not just color.Paid, pending, waiting, declined and failed each get a label and an icon, so status still reads for colorblind users and screen readers.

What exists and what doesn't. A 21-screen clickable prototype covering Split, Safe and Together, three earlier Figma prototypes, architecture docs and a case-study site. The message parsing and spending forecast are designed, not built, and I haven't tested the concept with users yet. If I took it further, I'd start by watching five friend groups settle a real shared bill.

02AI redesignHuman in the loopStartup funding

Capstack: funding data founders can trust

A funding directory kept current by an AI agent, with people in the loop for anything that could cost a founder a deadline.

My role
UX research, product design and front-end
Methods
Secondary research, personas, journey map, heuristic evaluation
Designed
AI-maintained listings, curator review, trust rules, weekly digest
Status
6-screen clickable prototype · not yet built or tested

The problem

Founders look for grants and accelerators across scattered directories, many of them out of date. Eligibility rules sit deep in long documents, and deadlines move.

A directory can make listings easy to scan and still dodge the real question: where does the data come from, and why should a founder trust it?

The redesign

An AI agent checks official program pages every day and proposes changes. How much it can do on its own depends on what a mistake would cost a founder.

Founders see what's verified, what's only been read by AI, and what's still in question. Curators review anything high-risk before it goes live.

How a listing stays current

  1. 1 · AgentReadsChecks official pages and program feeds daily, and notes the exact sentence each fact came from.
  2. 2 · RulesSorts by riskTypos publish on their own. Deadlines, amounts and eligibility wait for a person. Closing a program is never automatic.
  3. 3 · CuratorDecidesSees a before-and-after diff, the source quote, the agent's confidence and who's affected, then approves, edits, rejects or asks the program.
  4. 4 · FounderKnowsSees a status label on every fact, a weekly "what changed," and can report a mistake straight into the queue.

Inside the redesign

Tell founders what changed, with proof

Instead of vague alerts, a weekly digest sorted by what matters: deadlines that moved, new matches, and things Capstack couldn't confirm.

Every change cites its source. Unverified listings are labeled, and founders choose whether to see them before review.

When sources disagree, Capstack says so and tells founders to plan for the earlier date, rather than picking one.

Give the human enough to decide fast

Each proposed change shows the before and after, the exact source quote, and the agent's confidence with a reason, not just a score.

It also shows the stakes: how many founders are tracking it and how many might no longer qualify.

Every decision is logged by name and can be undone for 7 days. Founder reports jump the queue.

Autonomy matched to the cost of a mistake

The agent's freedom shrinks as the stakes rise. A typo fix publishes on its own. A deadline needs a person.

Closing a program is never automatic, because a wrong "closed" hides real money from founders, which is worse than a stale "open."

The status labels founders see each pair an icon with words, so they don't rely on color alone.

Key decisions

  1. Risk-tiered autonomy.The agent does more where mistakes are cheap and less where they cost a founder money or a deadline.
  2. Show the source, not just the answer.Each fact links to the sentence it came from, so founders and curators can check it in seconds.
  3. Never guess when sources disagree.Showing both dates and asking the program is slower, but it's honest, and founders can plan around it.
  4. Explain every match score.A bare "91% match" asks for blind trust, so each score lists the reasons behind it.

What exists and what doesn't. A 6-screen clickable prototype with fictional programs; the agent isn't built and nothing has been tested with founders yet. Next: test the agent on 30 real program pages to measure how often it reads deadlines and eligibility correctly, then run sessions with 5 founders and plant one wrong deadline to see whether the labels help them catch it.

03Concept projectConsumer AIExplainable suggestions

Closet: an outfit in one glance

An AI wardrobe for busy students. It suggests outfits only from clothes they already own, using their calendar and the weather, and explains every pick so they can grab one and go.

My role
Concept, interaction and visual design, prototype
Deliverables
16 interactive screens and states, Figma file with components, design spec
Tools
Claude Design, Figma
Status
Concept · not built or tested with users yet

The problem

A student's day can run from a 10 AM class to a 2 PM career fair to an evening meetup. Picking one outfit that works for all of it, the weather, and what's actually clean is a small decision that eats a busy morning.

Most style apps answer with shopping or a feed of other people's clothes. The question is simpler: what should I wear today, from what I own?

The idea

Closet reads the day's calendar and forecast, then picks three outfits from clean items in the wardrobe, with no prompt needed.

Every pick says why and where that reason came from. If it got the dress code wrong, one tap fixes it, and Closet says what it learned with an undo.

How a pick is made

  1. 1 · ContextReads the dayOnly the calendars the student turns on, plus the forecast. Event pages give the dress code when they state one.
  2. 2 · WardrobeUses what's cleanItems in the laundry or worn recently sit out. Nothing to buy, ever.
  3. 3 · PickExplains itselfThree outfits, each with reasons, the source of each reason, and a confidence level.
  4. 4 · StudentDecidesWear this, swap one piece, or Not this. Corrections change future picks, and every change can be undone.

Inside the prototype

Every reason names its source

Each pick lists why, and every reason carries a chip: from the event page, the forecast, your outfit log or your feedback.

Confidence is shown, not hidden. High when the event page states a dress code, low when Closet is guessing, and "Confirmed by you" once the student fixes it.

When it doesn't know, it asks one short question instead of guessing, and suggests a safe middle until it hears back.

One tap to fix, with an undo

Wrong dress code? Three chips fix it for this event, and Closet says it will remember.

Not this opens over the outfit being rejected, so the reason sticks to the right pick. Closet shows exactly what it will change before moving on.

The reasons are about clothes and plans only. Closet never comments on bodies, weight or fit.

Honest when the data runs out

No events, a laundry-day wardrobe, offline: each state says what's missing in plain words and still offers a next step.

Confidence drops to match. A weather-only pick is labeled low confidence, and offline reasons show when they were last checked.

It never blames the student for missing data. It asks, or works with what it has.

Value in one photo, on the student's terms

One photo of a closet rail becomes separate items. The student checks each tag, and anything the AI is unsure of is flagged in words.

Calendars are opt-in one by one. Work and personal can stay off, and photos are processed on the phone.

Style rules cover modest and cultural dress, and learned preferences are listed so they can be removed. Delete everything is one button.

Key decisions

  1. Delight first, then speed.The wardrobe doors slide open on the first few visits. After that they open on their own and land on today's pick, and Reduce Motion swaps the slide for a crossfade.
  2. Only what you own.No shopping and no feed. The trade-off: suggestions are only as good as the wardrobe Closet knows about, so setup has to be fast.
  3. Explain, then let people fix it.A pick without reasons asks for blind trust. Sources, confidence and one-tap corrections make it checkable.
  4. Say state in words, not just color.Clean, in laundry and worn recently each have a label, and every item is announced to screen readers with its state, like "Navy oxford shirt, clean."

What exists and what doesn't. A clickable prototype with 12 screens and 4 edge cases, a Figma file with editable layers, components and a linked prototype, and a design spec with measured contrast. Wardrobe recognition, calendar reading and the outfit logic are designed, not built, and the wardrobe is a fictional student's. Next: run the morning flow with 5 students and measure time from opening the app to "Wear this," and how often they trust or correct the reasons.

04Concept projectHealthcare AIGrounded summaries

Groundwork: the patient's story before the scan

A context assistant for radiologists. It pulls a patient's history, labs and prior reports into one view before the scan is read, links every line to the record it came from, and flags what's missing or contradictory. It never suggests a diagnosis.

My role
Research synthesis, interaction and visual design, prototype
Research
One in-depth interview with a radiology resident, 48 questions
Deliverables
Research board, 13 desktop screens and states, Figma file with a linked prototype
Status
Concept · synthetic records · not yet tested with radiologists

The problem

I interviewed a radiology resident about their day. A study arrives with a one-line request like "?Mass". The history, labs, prior surgery and old scans are spread across PACS, RIS, the hospital record and WhatsApp, and they hunt for them by hand.

In their words: "Sometimes the indication on the requisition form is too brief, making clinical correlation difficult." Meanwhile one finding can sit in a single image out of thousands.

The idea

Asked what a student should build, they said: "intelligent clinical context systems" that combine prior reports, labs, pathology and notes into one concise view.

Groundwork does that and stops there. Every line links to its source, gaps and conflicts are shown instead of filled in, and diagnosis stays with the radiologist.

How a case is prepared

  1. 1 · GatherReads the recordsThe request, notes, discharge summaries, labs, prior reports and uploaded outside reports for that hospital ID.
  2. 2 · GroundLinks every lineEach line points to its record and the exact words. Quotes stay word for word.
  3. 3 · FlagShows the gapsMissing items, records that disagree, and follow-ups recommended in past reports that never happened.
  4. 4 · RadiologistDecidesResolves conflicts, asks the referring team, and writes the findings. Nothing is sent without them.

Inside the prototype

One click from a line to the record

Every line carries a source chip. Clicking it opens the record on the right with the exact words highlighted, so checking takes a second, not a search.

Quotes stay quotes. Anything shortened links to the line it came from, and the panel says when the context was last checked.

"Looked for, not found" lists what's absent, including which systems aren't connected, so a gap isn't mistaken for a clean history.

Show both, let the radiologist decide

When records disagree, Groundwork doesn't pick a winner. It puts both side by side with who wrote each one and when.

The choice is logged with a name and can be undone. Both original records stay unchanged.

Asking is one step away. Groundwork drafts the question to the referring team from the gaps it found, and nothing goes out until the radiologist presses Send.

It answers about records, never about disease

The interview drew the line clearly: AI can assist, but it should never decide alone in complex diagnoses or oncology.

Asked for a diagnosis, it declines and points back to what the records actually say, with the source.

Diagnostic words are blocked from summaries: "likely", "suggestive of", "consistent with". If a source uses them, it's quoted, not paraphrased.

Context goes in; findings stay human

The clinical history arrives drafted from the records, with source numbers the radiologist can keep or drop.

Findings and impression are empty and labelled "You write this". Groundwork never drafts them.

Nothing gets lost in handoff. Open conflicts, unanswered questions and overdue follow-ups are listed before sending, and the consultant sees the same sources.

Loud only for safety, honest when thin

Most of Groundwork is quiet, because reading a scan takes full attention. Side, level and site mismatches are the exception: they take over the screen until someone confirms.

It never picks a side. Both confirm buttons look the same.

With only an emergency request, it won't write a summary from one line. It quotes the request and never guesses who an unidentified patient is.

Key decisions

  1. Records, not diagnosis.Staying on the "assist" side is a design choice, not a setting. It's narrower than an AI that reads images, and that's exactly why it's easier to trust and to check.
  2. Show conflicts instead of settling them.The trade-off: more for the radiologist to resolve. A silent pick could put the wrong history into a signed report.
  3. Say what's missing, and why.Each gap says where Groundwork looked and which systems it can't see, so missing data isn't mistaken for a clean history.
  4. Priority stays with the requesting team.Context tags describe the records, not how sick the patient is. Re-ranking the worklist would need clinical validation this concept doesn't have.

What exists and what doesn't. The research is one interview with one resident at one teaching hospital in India, so the six insights are leads to test, not proven needs. All patients and records are synthetic. Record retrieval, source linking and the word filter are designed, not built. Next: walk the same resident through the prototype (they offered to review it), add a consultant and a referring clinician, and measure time to find prior history and how often people open the source links.

How I work

Four rules I keep coming back to.

  1. Start at the decision.Find the exact moment someone needs help, then design backward from there.
  2. Go where people already are.Put help inside tools people already use before asking them to learn a new one.
  3. Show, then step back.Give the answer and the choice, then get out of the way.
  4. Make it checkable.Show sources and reasons, so people can catch a wrong answer instead of just accepting it.

Process

  1. Research
  2. Flows in Figma
  3. Working prototype
  4. Test with people
  5. Fix and repeat
